# Project Framing and Validation

## Purpose

Translate a request into a well-grounded understanding of the user's desired outcome and a way to judge whether the work is valuable. Do not mistake the requested implementation for the underlying need.

## Understand the need

Investigate, using available context and evidence:
- who experiences the problem or benefits from the result;
- what they are trying to accomplish;
- how they solve it today and where that approach fails;
- what triggered the request and why it matters now;
- what constraints, preferences, and trade-offs are explicit;
- what a useful result would change in practice.

Separate confirmed user statements from your interpretation. Record only confirmed facts as facts. Record consequential inferences as inferences and keep them revisable.

Do not make clarification a blocking dependency when context, research, or a reversible assumption can move the work forward. If a decision genuinely belongs to the user because it changes the Goal, taste, risk tolerance, or a material trade-off, present a concise recommendation and the decision needed.

## Define the outcome

Write a short outcome statement that captures:
- the user or beneficiary;
- the problem or opportunity;
- the intended change;
- the most important priority or constraint;
- what would count as a useful result.

Avoid overfitting the statement to a particular implementation. Keep secondary goals and priorities explicit rather than hiding conflicts.

## Test whether the proposed solution makes sense

For a product or service, consider as relevant:
- the target user and intensity/frequency of the pain;
- existing alternatives and why they are insufficient;
- the distinctive value of the proposed solution;
- how initial users or beneficiaries can be reached;
- why they would continue using or benefiting from it;
- operating, maintenance, and adoption costs;
- what evidence would justify continuing, changing, or stopping.

These are prompts, not mandatory sections for every project. For research, assess novelty, prior work, tractability, testability, and the value of resolving the question. For a creative project, assess the intended experience, audience, artistic constraints, and how quality will be judged.

## Define evaluation appropriately

Choose criteria that match the nature of the outcome:
- binary requirements: pass/fail checks;
- quantitative properties: metrics, thresholds, ranges, and measurement conditions;
- qualitative properties: rubric, exemplars, comparison, expert judgment, or user review;
- research: hypothesis, experimental validity, baselines, reproducibility, and strength of evidence;
- exploratory work: what has been learned, what has not been established, and what decision the result informs.

Do not invent false precision. Make important trade-offs visible. Acceptance criteria should protect the intended outcome, not merely make the chosen implementation easy to declare complete.

## Existing projects

Before reframing an active project, inspect its current Goal, decisions, constraints, recent results, and known rejected approaches. Identify what remains valid and what new evidence changes. Do not restart discovery from zero or resurrect an old direction without addressing why it was previously rejected.

## Deliverable

Produce a concise, revisable outcome statement and evaluation approach, then update the project's authoritative record. State material assumptions and unresolved questions without blocking reversible progress.
