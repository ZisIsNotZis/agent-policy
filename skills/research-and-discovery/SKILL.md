# Research and Discovery

## Purpose

Find a better-supported understanding of the problem and the most promising path toward the Goal. Research can produce code, a design, an experiment, a decision, a creative result, or a useful negative finding.

## Working approach

1. **Frame the decision.** State the problem, intended outcome, constraints, what is already known, and what decision or uncertainty research should change.
2. **Expose assumptions.** Separate observations, interpretations, hypotheses, and unknowns. Identify which assumptions matter most if wrong.
3. **Generate meaningful alternatives.** Consider different mechanisms, levels of abstraction, problem formulations, and relevant cross-disciplinary ideas. Seek materially different options, not cosmetic variations.
4. **Examine alternatives individually.** For each serious candidate, assess fit to the Goal, supporting evidence, failure modes, cost, dependencies, reversibility, and what would make it preferable.
5. **Challenge the leading idea.** Look for counterexamples, adversarial cases, hidden constraints, confounders, and evidence that would falsify the current explanation.
6. **Converge provisionally.** Choose the best-supported next direction. Explain why it currently leads and what uncertainty remains. Do not imply the choice is permanent.
7. **Discriminate with evidence.** Use the smallest experiment, prototype, analysis, benchmark, observation, or external investigation that can materially distinguish the leading explanations.
8. **Update the model.** Revise the problem framing, confidence, plan, and next question based on what was learned.

## Allocate exploration intelligently

Do not enumerate every conceivable branch. Do not commit deeply to the first plausible idea either. Direct attention toward branches with high expected value, high consequence if misunderstood, or high information gain relative to cost.

A practical heuristic:
- Explore more when uncertainty is high, mistakes are costly, and investigation is cheap.
- Act sooner when the choice is reversible, evidence is adequate, or waiting has a high opportunity cost.
- Run a small discriminating experiment before an expensive commitment when possible.
- Reopen a rejected branch when its original rejection reason changes or new evidence makes it competitive.
- Stop or redirect when additional exploration is unlikely to change a decision or improve the result enough to justify its cost.

A time box is a reassessment point, not an automatic stop. Do not impose arbitrary quotas on the number of ideas or experiments without understanding the problem.

## Reconsider the direction when

- repeated attempts fail for the same underlying reason;
- progress improves a local metric but not the actual Goal;
- evidence contradicts a central assumption;
- integration reveals that independently successful parts do not form a useful whole;
- a simpler or fundamentally different formulation becomes plausible;
- the expected value of continuing falls below the value of switching, pausing, or stopping.

When this happens, step back to the problem framing and alternatives. Do not merely intensify the same approach.

## Protect focused thinking

For open-ended work, create enough uninterrupted attention to reason about the problem. Avoid filling all available capacity with unrelated execution, status messages, or premature coordination. Keep necessary communication, but batch low-value interruptions where practical. This is a focus principle, not a rigid calendar rule.

## Record the useful result

Update the relevant project source of truth with:
- the question and why it mattered;
- assumptions that materially shaped the investigation;
- serious alternatives considered and the reason for the current choice;
- experiment or evidence and its conditions;
- result, interpretation, confidence, and limitations;
- rejected directions worth remembering and why;
- the next useful question or action.

Do not create a separate research log for every thought. Preserve enough to support decisions, reproducibility, and future reconsideration. A negative result is useful only when its conditions and implications are clear.

## Report

State what changed in the team's understanding, what the evidence supports, what remains uncertain, and what should happen next. Distinguish attempted, observed, inferred, tested, and verified claims.
